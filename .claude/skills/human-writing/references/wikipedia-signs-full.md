# Full list of signs from Wikipedia's "Signs of AI writing"

Source: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing (WP:AISIGNS), read in full, with the page's text current through October 2026. The page is descriptive, not prescriptive: these are observations, not rules, and any one sign alone is weak evidence. Part 1 applies to ordinary prose. Part 2 is Wikipedia-specific and is kept for completeness, for anyone writing or reviewing for Wikipedia.

The page's own warning applies to this whole file: "Please do not merely treat these signs as the problems to be fixed; that could just make detection harder." The real problem behind most signs is generic, unsupported content. Fix that first.

## Part 1. Signs that apply to ordinary prose

### Why they exist

- LLMs regress to the mean: the result tends toward the most statistically likely text that fits the widest variety of cases. Reinforcement learning then tunes output toward the most broadly palatable prose.
- Specific, unusual, nuanced facts are statistically rare, so the model replaces them with generic, positive descriptions. "Inventor of the first train-coupling device" becomes "a revolutionary titan of industry". The subject gets less specific and more exaggerated at the same time.
- The patterns are linguistic, so they appear whatever the subject is, which gives the text an identifiable "voice".

### Content signs

1. **Undue emphasis on significance, legacy and broader trends.** Words to watch: stands/serves as, is a testament/reminder, a crucial/pivotal/vital/significant/key role/moment, underscores/highlights its importance/significance, reflects broader, symbolizing its ongoing/enduring/lasting, contributing to the, setting the stage for, marking/shaping the, represents/marks a shift, key turning point, evolving landscape, focal point, indelible mark, deeply rooted, "distinction [matters/is important] because".
   - Applies even to the most mundane subjects, such as etymology or population data.
   - Sometimes opens with a hedge admitting the subject is of low importance, then claims importance anyway ("Though it saw only limited application, it contributes to the broader history of…").
   - Biology and nature topics: over-emphasis on links to the wider ecosystem, even when tenuous; belaboring conservation status, research and preservation efforts even if the status is unknown and no efforts exist.
   - Mid-2026 on: paired with hedging disclaimers that editorialize about why a distinction or nuance matters ("the figure should not be treated as an exact census, but the episode indicates…").
2. **Claims that a topic has influenced discussions about related topics.** Words to watch: has/have shaped conversations/discussions/debate about. Often a rule of three ("discussions about X, Y, and Z"). Situating a subject amid broader "debates" or "public discussions".
3. **Undue emphasis on notability, attribution and media coverage.** Words to watch: independent coverage, local/regional/national media outlets, music/business/tech outlets, trade publications, cited/featured/profiled in. The model proves importance by listing outlets and naming what kind of source they are, often attributing its own analysis to the source. Distinct from human press releases because the focus is on the characteristics of the sources themselves. More common in 2025 and later text.
4. **Undue emphasis on identification by primary or tertiary sources (2026 on).** Words to watch: databases/records/listings/filings/materials identify/list/describe [subject] as…, was identified by [source] as [fact], is listed in, his/her professional profile lists. Stilted stress on how websites, databases and records "identify" the subject.
5. **Undue emphasis on online presence.** Words to watch: maintains an active / a strong digital presence, social media presence.
6. **Superficial analyses.** Present participle (-ing) phrases tacked onto the end of sentences to comment on significance, recognition or impact. Words to watch: highlighting/underscoring/emphasizing, ensuring, reflecting/symbolizing, contributing to, cultivating/fostering, encompassing, enhancing, valuable insights, align/resonate with. Newer chatbots with web search may attach these to named sources ("Roger Ebert highlighted the lasting influence") whether or not the source says anything close.
7. **Vague expression of connection or association.** Words to watch: in connection with/to, connected with/to, in association with, associated with ("widely associated", "became associated with"). Say what the relationship actually is ("was CEO of", "taught science at").
8. **Vague attributions and overgeneralization of opinions.** Weasel wording: industry reports, observers have cited, experts argue, some critics argue, several sources/publications (when only a few are cited), "such as" before exhaustive lists. Exaggerating the number of sources, presenting one or two views as widely held, mentioning multiple "reviewers" while citing one person, or implying a list of examples is non-exhaustive when the source gives no sign other examples exist.
9. **Outline-like conclusions about challenges and future prospects.** Formula: "Despite its [positive words], [subject] faces challenges…", then a vaguely positive ending or speculation about initiatives that could help. Words to watch: Despite its… faces several challenges…, Despite these challenges, Challenges and Legacy, Future Outlook. The sign is the rigid formula, not any mention of challenges.
10. **"Awards and recognition" sections and "X and Y" headings.** Headers of the form "X and Y" are common; "Awards and recognition" (or just "Recognition") is near-ubiquitous in AI text, much more than in human writing, even versus promotional human writing.

### Language and grammar signs

11. **High density of "AI vocabulary" words.** One or two can be coincidence; a cluster is a sign. Overuse changes by model era:
    - 2023 to mid-2024 (GPT-4): additionally, boasts, bolstered, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, garner, intricate/intricacies, interplay, key, landscape, meticulously, pivotal, underscore, tapestry, testament, valuable, vibrant.
    - Mid-2024 to mid-2025 (GPT-4o): align with, bolstered, crucial, emphasizing, enhance, enduring, fostering, highlighting, pivotal, showcasing, underscore, vibrant.
    - Mid-2025 to mid-2026 (GPT-5): emphasizing, enhance, highlighting, showcasing, plus words tied to notability and media coverage.
    - Mid-2026 on (GPT-6): challenge, clearer, dependable, echoed, foster, leverage, matters, multifaceted, practical, prioritize, quietly, steady, universally; much more period and comma usage; shorter sentences around a strict 8 to 20 word limit.
    - Grok overuses superficially "scientific" words: causal, empirical, correlate; still overuses underscore as of 2026.
    - Also on the full watch list: deep dive, highlight (verb), meticulous, robust, showcase, additionally (especially opening a sentence), align with.
    - Take the list literally: a word being overused does not mean its synonyms are. Mind context ("underscore" can be a literal underline mark).
12. **Avoidance of basic copulatives.** serves as/stands as/marks/functions as/operates as/represents [a]; boasts/features/maintains/offers [a]; refers to. One study found over a 10% drop in "is" and "are" in 2023 academic writing. AI copyedits often "improve" text this way. Lead sentences may say "refers to" as if the article were about the word.
13. **Negative parallelisms.** Output sounds like it is correcting a misconception nobody held. Human writers use it too (myth-busting listicles), but it is stereotypically an AI sign.
    - "Not only… but…", "It is not just…, it's…".
    - "It's not…, it's…", "no…, no…, just…".
    - Reversed "Y rather than X", very common in recent output.
    - Also spread across sentences ("This isn't X. It's Y.") and in comments, reviews and replies.
14. **Leads treating lists or broad titles as proper nouns.** The first sentence defines the title as if it were a real entity ("The 'List of songs about Mexico' is a curated compilation…", "Historic churches of Lamont County refers to…").
15. **Rule of three.** adjective, adjective, adjective; or short phrase, short phrase, and short phrase. Used to make superficial analysis look comprehensive. Stronger where people would not bother with flourishes, such as edit summaries. Shows up in 2026 text too ("core elements such as…, magical weapons, and spirit of resistance").

### Style and formatting signs

16. Title case instead of sentence case in headings.
17. Overuse of boldface in prose, sometimes with asterisks (Markdown) misused.
18. Inline-header vertical lists (bold label, colon, sentence).
19. Wrong heading levels: a level-1 heading with the article title, or several level-1 headings.
20. Emoji at the start of section titles or list entries.
21. Curly quotation marks and apostrophes instead of straight ones.
22. Markdown where it does not render: asterisks for bold, number signs for headings, `---` or `----` dividers, Markdown link syntax, text wrapped in triple backticks.

### Communication intended for the user

23. **Collaborative communication and chatbot residue.** Words to watch: a Wikipedia-style article, I hope this helps, Of course!, Certainly!, You're absolutely right!, Would you like…, is there anything else, let me know, more detailed breakdown, here is a…, check/verify/confirm… before publishing/submitting. Includes prewriting or advice pasted as content ("In this section, we will discuss the background information…", "Delete this section before submission").
24. **Hedging disclaimers about source availability and usage.**
    - 2023 to 2024: didactic disclaimers ("important to note"), advice to an imagined reader on safety or controversial topics, disambiguating locale differences.
    - 2024 to 2025: claiming information is not publicly available, then guessing what it "likely" is. Words to watch: While specific details are limited/scarce…, not widely available/documented/disclosed, in the provided/available sources / search results, based on available information, does not by itself establish…. The guess and even the "not documented" claim are speculative.
    - Mid-2026 on: corrective framings. (a) "X should be treated as [speculative/symbolic] rather than [definitive/verified]", (b) "the available information does not establish [conclusion]", (c) "should not be confused with [other topic]" (least reliable). Very rare in AI output before mid-2026 (around the July 2026 GPT-5.6 release).
25. **Phrasal templates and placeholder text.** Fill-in-the-blank brackets left unfilled: [Your Name], [Describe the specific section…], (Add your channel URL here), "Evidence to be supplied", Mad-Libs-style templates.
26. **Prompt refusal.** "As an AI language model…", "I cannot offer medical advice, but I can…", apologies, with an alternative offered. Increasingly rare.
27. **Knowledge-cutoff statements (2022 to 2023).** "Up to my last training update", "as of my last knowledge update".

### Historical or older signs

28. **Promotional and advertisement-like language (2023 to 2025).** boasts a, vibrant, rich, profound, enhancing, showcasing, exemplifies, commitment to, natural beauty, nestled, in the heart of, groundbreaking, renowned, featuring, diverse array.
29. **Section summaries.** In summary, In conclusion, Overall; a "Conclusion" section; paragraphs ending by restating their core idea. Mostly older models, still seen after 2024 but less.
30. **Overuse of em dashes (2022 to September 2026).** More common than in non-professional human writing, used where humans would use commas, parentheses, colons, hyphens or en dashes; formulaic, "punched up" sales-style emphasis; often spaced. Weak alone. A July 2026 study found only Claude of the contemporary models used em dashes more than professional writers; ChatGPT used them less. From September 2026 newer models are often hard coded to avoid em dashes and then overuse commas and periods, so the variety of punctuation has dropped, and that low variety is now its own sign.
31. **Abrupt cut-offs.** Older tools stopped mid-text at the token limit. Not foolproof: bad copy and paste or copyright copying can cause it too.
32. **Lexical diversity / elegant variation.** Older models used a repetition penalty, so they swapped synonyms for the same thing (the subject appears as "the artists", "non-conformist artists", "like-minded artists", "the community"). Caveat: non-native English speakers may also avoid repeats (some Italian schools teach this), and separate pieces generated in isolation may not show it.
33. **Outdated access-date values** in citations. Newer chatbots seldom do it.
34. **Style drift.** A sudden shift to flawless, generic writing compared with the person's own earlier writing, or a writing style that tracks the era's LLM output. The reverse also holds: consistent habits with pre-2022 writing (frequent boldface, lists) suggest the newer text is less likely to be AI.
35. **Differences between LLMs.** Each model has its own idiolect. ChatGPT and Grok focus on broader context more than Gemini and Claude; Gemini and Claude are more concise than ChatGPT and Grok (Grokipedia articles are extremely long).
36. **Biases in content.** Output reflects training data and design, for example skew toward Western sources.

### Signs of human writing (use these)

- Text from before 30 November 2022 can be safely ruled out as AI.
- Ability to explain one's own editorial choices and mistakes.
- Simple is/has phrases: "there is a", "it has a".
- Plain words over stiff or euphemistic synonyms: wrote (not authored), moved (not relocated), used (not utilized), tried (not attempted), died (not passed away).
- Superlative or definitive statements: one of the best, is the only, was the first.
- Qualifiers and intensifiers: very, perhaps, tends to.
- Isolated wordy constructions: as a result of, in order to, all of the, a part of, the fact that.

### Ineffective indicators (do not treat as proof, do not strip out)

- Perfect grammar.
- Mixed casual and formal registers, or language that sounds both clinical and emotional.
- "Bland" or "robotic" prose.
- "Fancy", "academic" or "formal" prose in general (only the specific words above count).
- Transition words in isolation (Additionally, Consequently, Notably); only a few are overused, and human essays use them too.
- Unsourced content.
- Bizarre wikitext, or correct wikitext.

### Caveats from the page

- Do not rely only on AI detectors (GPTZero, Pangram and others). They beat chance but have non-trivial error rates, and are affected by paraphrasing, markup and spacing changes, and models they did not train on.
- Do not rely on your own judgment: a 2025 study found human ability to tell LLM text from human text no better than chance; one German-thesis study found 57% recognition of AI text and 64% of human text. A 2025 preprint found expert annotators correctly flagged 90% of AI text and wrongly flagged 3.3% of human text.
- Human speech and writing are being influenced by LLMs and becoming more similar, but LLM output is not becoming more like human writing.
- False accusations of AI use cause real harm; confirmation bias and Dunning-Kruger can cloud judgment.

## Part 2. Wikipedia-specific signs

Kept for completeness. Only relevant when writing or reviewing for Wikipedia.

### Markup irregularities

- Markdown asterisks and number signs; `---` dividers; Markdown link format `[url](text)` instead of wikilinks; text wrapped in triple backticks, often prefaced with "wikitext".
- Exposed internal citation data such as `contentReference[oaicite:1]` (unambiguous; each chatbot has its own pattern).
- Nonexistent categories and templates.
- Footnote jump characters (↩) around references.

### Citations

- Broken external links in a new article (especially if not in the Internet Archive). Watch for false positives: library-gated links, bot-mangled URLs, copy-paste truncation.
- Invalid DOIs and ISBNs (failed checksum); DOIs that lead to unrelated articles; fabricated scholarly references with real-looking DOIs.
- Book citations without page numbers or URLs; real books with cited pages that do not support the text.
- Incorrect or unconventional reference reuse, such as repeated re-use syntax after every full stop. A VisualEditor bug from 2018 to 2023 also produced irrelevant low-number PMID citations; these can look like AI but usually are not.
- `utm_source=openai`, `utm_source=chatgpt.com`, `utm_source=copilot.com`, `referrer=grok.com` in URLs. Near-proof of ChatGPT involvement in the citation, not necessarily the prose. Gemini and Claude use UTM parameters less.
- Named references declared in the reference list but unused in the body.
- Date placeholders (`{{CURRENTYEAR}}-xx-xx` in access-date or date fields) and URL placeholders (`INSERT_SOURCE_URL_##`, `example.com` article/news/source1 links).

### Comment-specific indicators (talk pages and discussions)

- Misquoted policies and invented shortcuts, or citing just "WP"; maintenance banners transcluded whenever mentioned.
- Lengthy comments split into titled sections in Markdown, plain text or subheadings.
- Downplaying LLM use ("I put some human effort in", "these are my thoughts"); asking critics for input on what to improve; dismissing origin concerns as speculation; urging critics to focus on content instead.
- Customer-service or clerk tone: "carefully reviewed", apologizing for any inconvenience, "please point out any issues and I will try to resolve them".
- Negative parallelisms, rule of three, "In summary", "In conclusion" in the comment itself.

### Edit summaries

Rigid formulae that appear almost nowhere before 2023. The more a summary departs from the pattern, the less likely it is AI. Abbreviations like "ce" are unlikely to be wholly AI.
- Older ones are first person, with AI vocabulary, emoji, list or Markdown formatting.
- Canned assurance of policy adherence: ensured that… adheres to, refined, enhanced, enriched, streamlined, improved, in compliance with, Wikipedia guidelines/style/standards, revised, verifiability, neutrality, neutral tone, encyclopedic tone, clarity, flow. Humans are brief and specific ("removed excessive links per MOS:OVERLINK").
- Procedural statements about what was preserved or avoided: preserved/preserving, retained/retaining, avoided/avoiding, ensured/ensuring, aimed/aiming to ("…while preserving the original meaning").
- Overemphasis on presence or reliability of citations: added sourced [information/content/section], added verified…, added [coverage/citations/references], improved attribution, with independent/secondary/third-party/peer-reviewed sources. A human says what content was added.
- Overemphasis on parameter, template and markup names ("corrected infobox parameters (image_size)", "added inline citations, internal links").
- Reference to an Articles for Creation review ("per reviewer feedback"), which a human would not feel the need to say.
- A chatbot preamble pasted into the summary ("Claude responded: That last sentence is the killer…").

### Miscellaneous

- "Submission statements" or reviewer notes in AfC drafts claiming notability and policy compliance, or disclosing AI use.
- Pre-placed maintenance templates, including AfC templates already set to "declined".
- Canned user pages: rigid headings (Welcome To My User Page!, About Me, My Interests, Let's Connect!), emoji, bold (including Markdown attempts), "Happy Editing! 🚀".
- Permissions gaming with throwaway AI rewrites (use the sign in one direction only: do not accuse someone of it just for adding a lot of AI text).
- Hidden creator-facing comments in the page source: instructions on adding sources or images if available, uploading to Wikimedia Commons. Not the same as the boilerplate comments built into some templates.
- Wikipedia-specific unblock requests and ANI reports written by an LLM, with a clerk-like tone and invented evidence lists.
